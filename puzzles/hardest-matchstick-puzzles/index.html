Matchstick

The hardest matchstick puzzles: move one match

These are the ones that make people say it can't be done. Every equation is wrong, one match fixes it, and there's only one match that does. Six at the end need two. Play them here, open the answer under each one, or print the set.

By ManextApps6 min readUpdated 6 November 2026

Puzzle L1 (three numbers)

Move exactly one match to make the equation true.

Tap a match to pick it up, then tap a dashed spot to put it down. The board checks the math straight away, and it accepts any true equation, not only the one in the key. Hint goes in three steps: where the match comes from, which match it is, and where it lands. With a keyboard, Tab to a match and press Enter, then Tab to a spot and press Enter again.

New to these? Start with the 30 easier matchstick puzzles first. They use the same rules and teach the digit changes these ones lean on.

The rules

  • Move exactly one match, or two in the last section. No adding, no removing, no breaking, no diagonal matches.
  • Digits look like a digital clock. A 1 is two upright matches, a 7 is a 1 with a match on top.
  • Signs are matches too. A minus is one flat match, a plus is a flat one and an upright one, an equals sign is two flat ones.
  • The result must be a real equation: numbers joined by plus and minus, exactly one equals sign, both sides equal, and no number that starts with a zero.

What makes these hard

A puzzle isn't hard because the numbers are big. It's hard because so many moves look right. Every answer below says how many single moves turn the puzzle into a proper equation that's still false. Those are the dead ends.

On the easier page there are 3 to 13. Here there are between 38 and 158, and exactly one move that works.

The set comes in three kinds:

  • Three numbers. Two signs on one side means the match can go to a sign you weren't looking at.
  • Sign swaps. The fix moves the equals sign itself, so the two sides of the equation change places.
  • Big numbers. Four digit answers full of 8s and 9s, where almost every match can move somewhere.

How I picked them

I didn't write these by hand. A small program started from thousands of true equations, undid one match move in every possible way, and kept each false equation it got. Then the same engine that runs the board above tried every single move on each one and kept only those with exactly one true result. That left a little over 56,000 puzzles.

The two match puzzles were made the same way, two moves back from a true equation. For those, the engine tried every pair of moves, and it kept a puzzle only when exactly one pair works and no single move does.

From those I took the ones with the most dead ends, preferred matches that travel a long way, and allowed each kind of move only once, so you won't solve two puzzles with the same trick. Anything already in my app or on the easier page was left out. The site also checks every answer again on each update with a second solver written separately, just to be safe.

How to crack the hard ones

Staring doesn't work on these. Counting does. Here's the order I'd go in.

1. Work out the gap

Add up both sides as they stand and write down the difference. Then think about what a single move can do to it. A 9 that gives up a match becomes a 3 or a 5, so it drops by 6 or 4. An 8 that loses its middle match becomes a 0. Each of those changes is worth a fixed amount, and only a few of them fit the gap.

2. Flip a minus in your head

Turning a minus into a plus adds twice the number after it. That's a big jump, and it's the move most people miss in long sums.

Take L8, 59 = 16 + 18 − 19. The right side is 15. Make that minus a plus and it's 16 + 18 + 19, which is 53. Now the left side is 6 too big, and a 9 that gives up its upper left match turns into a 3. That's exactly the match the plus needed.

3. Read the equals sign somewhere else

When nothing adds up, take one match off the equals sign and lay it on a minus. The equals sign becomes a minus, the minus becomes an equals sign, and the equation splits in a new place. In S1, 17 − 15 + 10 = 8 is wrong, but 17 = 15 + 10 − 8 is right. Try reading the equation with the equals sign at each minus before you move a single match.

4. Big numbers: guess the answer first

With four digit numbers, don't hunt for the match. Do the sum. In B6, 1993 = 995 − 958, the right side is 37, which is nowhere near. With a plus it's 1953, and 1993 is only one digit away from that. Now there's just one question left: which match turns a 9 into a 5 and also gives the minus its upright match.

5. Use the donor list

Only a few digits can lose a match and still be a digit: 7 becomes 1, 8 becomes 0, 6 or 9, 9 becomes 3 or 5, and 6 becomes 5. Find every one of those in the puzzle. In every puzzle here except the sign swaps, the match comes from one of them, which takes you from dozens of options to a few.

Two matches

These six play by slightly different rules: you'll move exactly two matches, not one. Everything else is what you're used to, and each one has a single true equation two moves away. There isn't a single one that a lone match can fix, so don't stop at the first true looking change.

Two moves give a lot more freedom, which is why they're hard. The trick is to treat them as one change of two digits or signs: work out the gap, then look for a pair of changes that closes it.

Questions

Are these really the hardest matchstick puzzles?

They're the hardest I could find under clear rules. Out of a little over 56,000 one move puzzles with a single answer, these have the most moves that look right but aren't. Hard is personal, though. If you have one that beats these, send it to me.

Is there only one answer to each?

Yes, under the rules above. A program tried every possible move on every puzzle and found exactly one true equation each time. If you allow a not equal sign or a match laid diagonally, you can find others, but those break the rules.

Why do some answers have four digit numbers?

Because the numbers had room for it. 1953 = 995 + 958 is just as true as 3 + 4 = 7, and big sums full of 8s and 9s give the most places to put a match.

Can the equals sign really move?

Yes. An equals sign is two matches, so taking one leaves a minus, and putting it on a minus makes a new equals sign. It's one match moved, which is all the rules ask for.

Is there an app with more?

Yes. My Matchstick Puzzle app for Android has 550 levels, including two match moves, add or remove puzzles and shapes, plus a daily puzzle. It follows the same rules and works offline.

If you get stuck, go back to the gap. Work out how far off the equation is, and the match usually shows itself.
